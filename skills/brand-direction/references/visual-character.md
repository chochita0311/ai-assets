# Judging visual character

Use these comparisons to compose a new direction, find what an earlier frame did better, or test which choices carry over to another task. Pick the comparison that answers the open question and show the result in the working screen.

## Find what made the earlier frame work

Open the favored frame and its challenger at the same size, appearance and point in the task. Take in the whole screen at a size where copy is hard to read. Describe what the eye meets first and second. How much space does the accent occupy? Does the artwork feel embedded in the environment or enclosed in a separate panel? Look again at actual size for the lighting, edges, crop or typographic gesture responsible for that effect. Choose the observations that explain this pair.

Record the reviewer's reaction separately from your explanation. “I like that photograph” can mean the image itself or the way an object appears out of darkness. Use the stated level of preference. If it is ambiguous, use the next specimen to distinguish the readings.

## When different briefs look the same color

Put the opening screens side by side at the same scale, without captions. Repeat with the first meaningful action completed. Identify the largest fields in each frame, including margins, images, empty space and result panels. Inspect individual screens at reading size. Note where the hue, lightness and intensity repeat, which material belongs to the subject and which surface comes from the shared layout. Check a narrow view where a crop or a newly visible result can change the balance.

Locate the repeat before changing it. Open the product directly and then view its thumbnail in the collection. The gallery mount, the application canvas below the last control, the material behind an object inside a photograph, and the face of an issued ticket can each produce a similar cast. Cover the image briefly to locate the broad field, then restore it to judge the edges together. When an earlier set feels freer, look for both a broad color plane and a restrained page in it. Trace what the color or the empty space lets someone do in each.

Test a repeated ground against another plausible mounting of the same material. If both the page color and the arrangement could account for the reaction, compare identical images on both grounds, then compare arrangements on one ground; preserve the displayed subject scale or report how it changed. Distinguish the task of comparing two things from studying one closely. Keep a warm field when it holds the material and its working labels together; a calm white sheet or an uninterrupted vivid field can earn its place on the same terms. Recheck the issued state and surrounding empty space before rendering the rest of the set. The brief and the reviewer’s reaction determine the choice; equal shares of colors across products would answer a different question.

Look for works that solve different color problems. A climbing wall can carry a route through several objects of one color. A pattern maker can reserve a colored line for the cut that matters. Inspect the actual work and record which object or surface carries the color. Try both a large color field and a restrained treatment when the tasks warrant them. Give the next action a specific relationship to that choice, such as the same route color following a selected hold, or a cut line moving with the seam allowance.

To resolve a dispute about the amount of color, keep the task, values, type, image, layout and viewport steady and change where the color extends. Identify any repair shared by both versions before attributing a difference to the palette. Compare legibility, meaning and atmosphere separately. Keep labels, line styles or other cues for a state that needs to be read without hue. If pixel coverage helps explain a recurring cast, state the color range, denominator, viewport and state. A measured area can diagnose repetition. Taste and ease of use need their own evidence.

## Make one fact stand out

A person admiring a photograph, finding a destination or preparing equipment has a different reason to look. Name the object or fact the person needs now and ask a reader what actually caught their eye before showing the rationale. Keep the reviewer's judgment about the mood alongside the answer. An attractive flower can be the right focal point on entry while the recorded observation deserves precedence after saving.

Locate the color at the relevant edge. To test a photographic surround, keep the same image, crop, lettering and note, and change just its neighboring ground. To test the prominence of light lettering, keep the ink, field, size and state fixed and compare two available weights in the actual script. Inspect the rendered strokes at the smaller viewport. The foreground-to-background luminance ratio stays constant in a weight-only comparison. A reader can still find the heavier word easier to locate, or prefer a lighter word when scanning adjacent numbers. Record which question the comparison answered. If the new surface also changes lightness or the ink also changes, say so.

For an operational accent, try an ordinary item selected while a different item carries an exception. Identify both from the same frame. Check the reverse selection and the result. A persistent word or shape can keep the exception attached to its owner while an outline or pointer follows the person's selection. If the problem is finding one value within a coherent family, compare a local intervention on that value with reducing the whole field. Retain a quiet, same-hue treatment when position, weight and space already make the necessary fact easy to locate. An individual preference for more contrast in one product does not set the palette for the collection.

## Assign the ink and question the line

When “the colors feel off” could describe the artwork, the page or the lettering, hold the picture, crop, type sizes and state still. Find which words inherit the subject’s accent. Try giving ordinary words a neutral ink of similar lightness and leaving the accent on the fact a person needs. Keep a treatment in which the repeated hue deliberately unifies the piece. If a white or darker slip is a real rival, render it too and identify both changes to its field and its ink. Ask which version someone wants to inhabit and which number, choice or destination they can locate. Those choices can differ. A soft or vivid ground can carry the atmosphere while keeping the only change of state small; quiet work can deserve a single ink throughout.

When the problem is the surrounding field, place the same subject and controls on plausible grounds at comparable lightness where feasible. Record any residual lightness difference. Look at both the small object and the large empty margin at the actual viewport. A cool ground might place a scene on ice, while a grey ground makes the rings feel self-contained. A vote for either is a situated reading. To investigate a recurring layout, reduce the first and finished surfaces side by side and trace the object, the action and the edges that connect them. Temporarily hide one rule with its space and type positions intact. Can the reader still name where a measurement starts and ends, what two items belong together or where the result begins? Keep lines that explain those relationships. If a rule survives only as a familiar page outline, try letting the object's contour, a change of ground or an uninterrupted text passage organize the space. Inspect the whole result again at phone width.

## The color that appears on selection

Look at an object before selection, while chosen and after the action takes effect. Identify the immediate surface behind its mark and the larger field around that surface. A hue that looks lively on a swatch can resemble rust or a warning on the actual control. Preserve the user's judgment for the named scene. Check what nearby colors already mean. A bay can be available, selected or occupied by the person at different stages.

Where the cause is open, render the original and another ink on the same silhouette, then expand that same ink onto a local field and reverse the figure if needed for legibility. When comparing another bright field, record any change in the foreground ink or lightness that comes with it. Hold the item, text, shape and neighbors fixed for the ink-only comparison. Ask separately where the selection is easiest to find, which treatment belongs to the brand, and what a person thinks the accent *means*. An observation about salience cannot establish that the meaning is clear. Keep a label or other legible cue when hue alone leaves two states ambiguous.

## When a short instruction wraps

Check the small copy beside the composition at the width that produced the complaint. See which words actually occupy each line. Try the same sentence at a natural measure and in a narrow block with meaningful phrase boundaries. Preserve the amount, object and verb. Compare the wide view with the last width before a stacked layout and with enlarged text. Two alternatives can be indistinguishable on a phone while separating a short auxiliary verb on desktop. A deliberate two-line instruction can balance a narrow column. The same sentence may read well on one line when space permits. Give the phrase room to wrap again when text enlargement makes it wider than the viewport.

## Color at the point of contact

Judge a combination where its colors actually meet. A swatch of violet tells little about a loaf on a violet mount or a violet glint inside dark shell. Look at both materials in the frame. Trace their shared edge, the surrounding area, the direction of the light, and which surface appears to carry the pigment. Ask separately whether the colors belong together and whether their meeting is surprising. A familiar combination can be convincing but forgettable. An unexpected pair can hold through a shared reflection, a repeated interval, a deliberate clash against one quiet edge, or the work of the material itself.

When the cause is unclear, keep the item, photograph, crop, text, task, focus and viewing size identical. Cross two plausible hues with two placements or extents when either could explain the reaction. Compare pairs that differ on one question and inspect the original beside them. Changing ink contrast or surface lightness along with hue limits the attribution. Use the comparisons to identify the actual surface a reviewer wants to change. Leave room for an entire vivid field when its relationship holds at that scale. Preserve a direct dislike on the named product even if another reader likes the experiment.

For a hand-drawn intervention, look closely at an actual mark before designing one. Where does the stroke begin and release? What in the image, letter, edge or movement gives it a place? An alteration mark can follow a specific cut. A purely visual stroke can answer a curve across empty space without pretending to report a measurement. Try removing the stroke, or moving the very same stroke to a different neighbor. Inspect at reading size on a phone. If the alternative is indistinguishable, leave the preference open. If the displaced mark falsely points to another object or syllable, repair the placement even when the composition looks lively. Follow the gesture into the actual crop on the issued artifact. A complete sweep can echo a repeating curve on the working sheet while its cut-off middle looks like a correction drawn over the finished image. Put the whole sheet and detail side by side before deciding which surface carries the gesture. On a finished artifact, decide whether a working trace still carries meaning or the craftsmanship is better felt in the clean result.

## Compose around the work

### Contemporary work and the reader's attention

A recent exhibition can reveal an older work worth studying. Record who made the work, when it was made or released if known, and what a later exhibition, award or update actually dates. An observed choice in one commission supports a claim about that commission. A claim about a wider trend needs corroboration across independent work. For an exploratory brief, a specific, well-observed work can be enough. Look beyond a named artist, follow a curator to the maker and let an older precedent with a closer working problem stay in contention.

Ask what requires a second look, what needs sustained attention and what someone must find on arrival. Decide what can carry the brand between those moments. A dramatic shadow can explain which object blocks light. A large fraction in the same position could draw attention away from the equal lengths a student needs to compare. Render both interpretations with the same explanation or question. Give a reader a concrete relationship to find. Agreement that a page looks calm says little about whether that relationship can be found.

When two uses of the very same object compete, put both questions to the same treatments. Someone at the door may want the time on a festival pass to lead. Someone saving that pass as a souvenir may value a broad photograph. The direction can keep a recognizable crop, type rhythm or material in both. State which use governs the proposed product surface and which other use still has a case.

### Anchor the composition

Start with an ordinary decision, its material and what changes for the person. Identify the object that can organize the whole scene. A printer chooses a page on a flat sheet, while a reader encounters it next to a different page in a bound book. A venue can organize a visitor's route by the physical heights of the floors. Find a maker's still, spread or working sequence that reveals a comparable relationship. Inspect the original at reading size. Describe what has to be invented for this product.

Give the actual words, figures and small labels different jobs. Choose a face, scale, measure, line break, alignment and neighboring element for the product fact that should lead. A timing board can put the leading team or the margin first. Test a reversal where the team changes and the absolute margin stays identical. A type name that applies only to a Latin wordmark says little about the Korean heading or the digits used at the moment of decision. Inspect the rendered glyphs for each relevant script and the fallback for mixed-script labels. Follow the [font terms](font-use.md) for any face that travels with the study.

Sketch another way to organize the same task before committing to a familiar picture-and-controls layout. Work in neutral values or compare small grayscale renders when color already succeeds. Ask where the eye enters, which edges align with meaning, what the user acts on and where the consequence appears. At reading size, check details the silhouette hides. Color reduction can expose a repeated arrangement. A distinctive outline alone does not tell you whether a person can use the product.

Carry an identifiable relationship into a second use with different content or a different job. A performance title might give way to a seat and time on an admission ticket. For a print proof, the full photograph and its crop have different purposes. Keep what identifies the product and change what leads according to the new task. Challenge the trade-off in place when enlarging a detail, retaining a source trace or moving a label could improve one judgment at the expense of another. A mobile version can need new space for a floor label or a different view of the same object.

## When two identities share the material

A strong picture or an unusual industry can make ordinary layouts look original. For that specific doubt, find the nearest neighbor: two services that use the same object or words for different jobs. Give them the same source photograph, text and factual fields. Decide what each person needs to do with that material. Look for the change in the relationship: a portrait can identify a speaker in a transcript or remain large enough to judge a subtitle against the scene.

Try the choice on an unseen item and a different surface the product might issue, such as a saved record or a printed card. Hide the brand name when it gives the answer. Inspect which clues also belong to the neighboring product. When color or lettering still gives an easy answer, neutralize that particular clue or deliberately give it to the rival. Report what was held steady and what still varies. An uncertain attribution is a useful result.

Check the claim the treatment now makes. A beautiful crop on a saved record may show a different part of the object than the one selected, and a large quotation mark may give an edited sentence the appearance of verbatim speech. Follow the exact selection or words into the result and compare it with the source. Preserve the expressive choice when it can also tell the truth. When two tasks favor different hierarchies, identify who needs each view and let the product change emphasis at that moment.

## Make an authored variation

Name one relationship to carry forward and one freedom the designer still has. Start from the strongest frame. Develop the free part with an actual visual intervention, such as changing the subject and angle of a photograph while keeping its lighting and scale in the composition. Use another admired work to sharpen that intervention when useful. Show the result inside the working screen.

For example, an evening music program might feel intimate because a tightly cropped performer almost disappears into the unlit stage. A brighter portrait with a large concert-date panel could make the date easier to find while changing the feeling to that of a listings service. A different performer caught in the same side light would test whether intimacy travels with the photographic treatment. The original portrait, its placement, and its lighting are three distinct choices.

## Challenge the recommendation

Look first without the concept caption, with both full frames visible. Say where the intended impression grew stronger, weaker or stayed the same, and point to what produced it. Then inspect the ordinary task at reading size. A palette match, consistent tokens, clear metadata or a clever explanation of a graphic supports only the specific claim it demonstrates. If the challenger loses the quality the reviewer values, keep the earlier frame in contention and carry useful local improvements separately. Where the comparison is genuinely unresolved, present that choice as open.
