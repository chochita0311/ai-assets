# Reviewing a set of studies

Write each ordinary user's task and a meaningful follow-through before designing. Choose different constraints when testing versatility. A calm gallery and a time-critical board deserve different hierarchies. For each, render the entry and the moment a real decision takes effect, including the viewport that puts pressure on it.

When independent reviewers are requested and available, give fresh reviewers the brief and unannotated rendered frames. Withhold the author's rationale, expected verdict and other reviews. Ask what first drew their eye, what action they would take, what changed in the following state, and where clarity or visual character falls away. Request a concrete observation anchored to the frame. An unresolved choice or a judgment that a study needs rework is a valid result. With a single author, describe the check as an author review.

For a collection, a reviewer of a small subset can find a missed detail while reviewers of the whole set can compare how well each identity survives the task. Choose staffing to fit the request. Let each reviewer record a first judgment before sharing critiques. Withhold the author’s preferred answer and other ballots. Record which frames and viewports each reviewer inspected. Reviewers of the same render share a visual source.

Ask which visible choice makes this work recognizable, where it thins out, and what would be lost in the competing treatment. A smaller photograph may reveal a result sooner while sacrificing its material presence. Compare the set itself for repeated layouts that burden different tasks. Check whether the identity persists in a dense passage, a selected item, an unavailable action or another relevant working state. Keep perceptual judgments distinct from runtime verification. Exercise the controls, keyboard path, calculations and recovery that actually support the brief.

In the working state, identify the new fact the person needs first. If the opening photograph or title still dominates a confirmation, try giving its space to the result while retaining a recognizable detail of the identity. If the decision affects two items or periods, check that both effects can be read together. On a narrow screen, inspect which object a crop or label actually identifies.

If a sequence is presented as one person's path, follow one selection through the real action and capture its consequence. A correct example from another route can make a working transition appear broken. When the task requires switching items, include the switch or the intervening state with the sequence. Mark standalone sample states as such. Keep a reviewer's observation about the frames and the result of exercising the control separately.

When the collection shares a color cast, compare the opening sheet with the sheet of working states before discussing individual swatches. Check for a recurring division of image and controls even if the dominant hues differ. Use the [color comparison](visual-character.md#when-different-briefs-look-the-same-color) to make a disputed area or surface inspectable.

For a disputed material or mark, ask for first impressions of harmony, distinctiveness and quality of finish separately. Ask where in the frame each judgment arose, including cases where an unusual choice works or an elegant one feels interchangeable. Withhold the rationale until first readings are recorded. In a controlled comparison, allow “both”, “neither” and “cannot see the difference”. A vote on where a color belongs answers a different question from a vote on whether a reviewer wants that color in this product. Check a suspected false cue against the actual action or issued object.

For disputed emphasis, ask readers to locate the actual item and read its value before asking which treatment they prefer. When appropriate, give the same alternatives two tasks, such as finding a destination and reading its departure time. A reader may prefer different treatments. Keep the order of alternatives independent of their origin. Report the specific change, the unchanged cues, any competing first impression and any tie. A correctly readable still and an account of what drew a reviewer's eye carry different evidence from a timed or observed user task.

For a type or composition critique, let reviewers first inspect reduced monochrome views and then the original opening, decision and second surface at reading size. Ask which product fact leads in each and what the move to another surface gains or loses. A label the reviewer could read, a known subject or an illustration can reveal the identity in grayscale, so describe this as an inspection of hierarchy. To test an uncertain choice, render the same text, state and viewport with the two proposed arrangements and identify any other differences.

When rival readings would change a decision, make both proposals usable and render the same content, action, viewport and focus condition. Give the comparisons neutral names and vary their order across reviewers. After first judgments are saved, supply the strongest case for each treatment without vote totals. Ask each reviewer for a preference or abstention, the best counterargument, what changed their mind and when the other treatment would win. Label this an informed second reading. Report the count with its denominator, abstentions and any dissent. A majority can express a preference. Verify claims about facts and controls against the working specimen, and claims about people against observed use. A specialist’s objection can warrant a trial even when a whole panel liked the original.

For a disputed transfer, keep the unmarked reference frames and candidate frames separate. If testing attribution within a pair, use the same subject and factual content for the candidates, hide names that reveal the origin, vary the presentation order and allow “can't tell”. Record the identifying cue and a competing explanation before revealing origin. Count decisions by reviewer and condition. A forced choice among two known options supports a narrower claim than recognition in the wild.

When both sides make a valid case, ask the same readers to judge the same alternatives for the other person's task as well. A result can reverse when the question changes from finding a detail to locating it on the whole. Check a look-alike with a deliberately misplaced detail if mere stylistic resemblance could give a false pass. Keep that diagnostic specimen separate from a proposed product change.

Adjudicate a consequential criticism against the screen or interaction. Repair a supported failure in its own study, render that same condition again, and record which reviewer or author checked the revision. Report the evidence and limits per study. Good results across self-authored examples support the tested situations; human preference and repeatable independent creation require their own observations.
